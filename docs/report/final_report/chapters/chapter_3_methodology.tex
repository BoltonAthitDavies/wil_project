\chapter[METHODOLOGY]{Methodology}
\label{chap:methodology}

This chapter describes how the project answers the problem set out in
\cref{chap:introduction}: its requirements and scope, the system architecture,
the front ends and back end as they were actually built, the simulation and real
platforms, the two experiments that structure the evaluation, and the metrics.

\textbf{Reporting cutoff.} Every implementation claim in this chapter describes
the system as used for the reported runs. Components are labelled
\status{implemented}, \status{partial}, \status{blocked} or \status{planned};
nothing planned is described in the present tense as though it existed.

\section{Problem, Requirements and Scope}
\label{sec:problem-scope}

The engineering problem is to localize a warehouse AMR with cameras and an IMU,
without floor infrastructure, in an environment whose contents move. The
company's present LiDAR-and-QR-code navigation is accurate but expensive to
install and inflexible (\cref{sec:background}); a camera-based replacement is
attractive only if it stays accurate when goods, pallets and people move
through the field of view, which is exactly the case standard visual SLAM
assumes away.

\textbf{Requirements.} The objectives of \cref{sec:objectives} are made testable
as four requirements.
\begin{enumerate}[label=R\arabic*., leftmargin=3em]
  \item The system shall estimate the planar pose of the robot, $(x, y,
    \psi)$, from stereo images and inertial measurements alone, without
    external infrastructure such as floor markers or beacons.
  \item The system shall construct a two-dimensional occupancy grid of the
    environment and shall subsequently relocalize the robot against that grid
    in read-only mode, given a known initial pose.
  \item The system shall publish the \texttt{map}~$\rightarrow$~\texttt{odom}
    transform and the \texttt{/map} occupancy grid in the form consumed by
    Nav2. Every correction shall pass through a validation stage --- the
    correction gate and the pose gate --- that rejects corrections
    inconsistent with the accumulated odometric drift.
  \item The system shall operate in real time at a replay rate of 1.0 on the
    development laptop, an MSI Katana GF66 11UG, and shall record for every run
    the per-frame processing latency, the fraction of frames processed, the
    processor utilization and the peak memory usage.
\end{enumerate}
\Cref{tab:targets} gives the numeric target each requirement is read against.
The map targets and the availability and continuity targets were fixed before
any map or relocalization run was scored.

The trajectory targets are stated at the working speed of
\SI{1.5}{\metre\per\second}. ATE is evaluated in three dimensions, the stricter reading of R1's planar pose,
because height and tilt errors still enter the estimate the planner receives;
the floor-plane figure is reported beside it in \cref{chap:results}. The RPE
target was fixed after the trajectory results were known, which can only cost the project passes: the filter on the
visual systems' inertial noise model, and ORB-SLAM3 when its initialization
frame step is counted, miss it. \flag{The real recordings were driven at
\SI{0.28}{\metre\per\second}, not \SI{1.5}{}, so the real-robot RPE is
measured under an easier condition than the target assumes
(\cref{sec:exp1-real}).}

\textbf{Site.} The real recordings are made on the first floor of the
Gensurv building, along hand-driven routes with no scripted moving objects and
unchanged lighting; an optical tracker following a marker on the robot
provides a planar ground-truth reference (\cref{sec:real-site}).

\textbf{Two domains.} Every experiment is defined once for two domains, a
Gazebo simulation of the warehouse and on the real robot. The simulation
supplies exact ground truth, exact calibration and repeatable dynamic scenes;
the real robot supplies the sensors, the lighting and the physics the project
is ultimately about. The two domains are never pooled.

\textbf{What is excluded.} Path planning and motion control are outside the
project: the pipeline delivers a pose and a map to Nav2, which plans and drives
(\cref{fig:scope-architecture}).

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

\subsection{Data flow and rates}

\Cref{fig:scope-architecture} shows the system. Stereo images and inertial
measurements enter the visual-inertial front end, ORB-SLAM3 or VINS-Fusion,
at \SI{30}{\hertz} and \SI{200}{\hertz}; either one supplies the odometry, and
RTAB-Map is the one back end. A camera shim hands RTAB-Map the stereo pair at
\SI{4}{\hertz}; RTAB-Map localizes the robot against a prior map built once and
issues a correction at about \SI{1}{\hertz}. Every correction passes the
correction gate, which refuses one inconsistent with the drift the odometry can
have accumulated, and the pose gate, which holds the planner until a
localization is trusted; the gated pose reaches Nav2 at \SI{20}{\hertz}.

The system runs at three speeds on purpose. Estimation is fast: the front end
must follow every camera frame and IMU sample or frames are dropped. Driving is
steady: the controller and the correction gate run at \SI{20}{\hertz}.
Remembering is slow: recognizing a place is expensive and places do not change
quickly, so RTAB-Map and the planner run at \SI{1}{\hertz}, the costmaps and
pose gate at \SI{2}{\hertz}. The pose Nav2 reads therefore stacks two answers to
where the robot is: the front end's smooth estimate, which drifts slowly, and
RTAB-Map's latest correction, which is right but arrives only now and then. It
moves smoothly between fixes and steps when a fix lands
(\cref{sec:frames-contract}). ORB-SLAM3's own Atlas and loop closer remain
active inside the front end and are evaluated as one of its switches in
Experiment~1. The two experiments of \cref{sec:experiments} each test one part
of this flow:

\section{The Pipelines as Implemented}
\label{sec:pipelines-implemented}

\subsection{ORB-SLAM3 front end}
\label{sec:orb-method}

The project provides a purpose-built ROS~2 Humble wrapper around the pinned
ORB-SLAM3 core (\cref{fig:orb-pipeline}). Approximate-time synchronization
pairs left and right images with a \SI{0.02}{\second} slop. Raw and compressed
transports are supported. Image callbacks place stereo pairs in a one-frame
queue; a worker thread calls \texttt{TrackStereo()} so blocking visual tracking
does not starve the \SI{200}{\hertz} IMU subscription. If tracking falls behind,
the older pending image is discarded and the drop count is reported. The
feature extractor runs on the CPU or, selected by configuration, on the GPU
through a run-time-compiled kernel.

The simulation configuration requests \num{1500} ORB features, eight pyramid
levels, a 1.2 scale factor, initial FAST threshold 20 and fallback threshold 7;
the real-rig configuration requests \num{2000} per camera at
$1920\times1080$. \Cref{tab:orb-params} collects the simulation values. No
controlled feature-count or FAST-threshold sweep is complete.

\subsection{VINS-Fusion front end}
\label{sec:vins-rtab-method}

VINS-Fusion uses stereo features and inertial measurements in a sliding-window
nonlinear estimator (\cref{fig:vins-pipeline}). It propagates existing features
with pyramidal KLT optical flow and detects new ones only to replenish the
tracked set up to its budget. The ROS wrapper synchronizes the image topics and
uses separate callback groups for images, IMU and feature messages. The
simulation configuration processes every image (\repo{image_skip: 1}), uses two
cameras and IMU, fixes the simulated extrinsics, targets \SI{25}{\hertz} feature
publication, and uses 10-pixel keyframe parallax. Image queues were increased to
100 and the IMU queue to \num{2000} to reduce losses during initialization and
backlog. On the real rig the configuration additionally masks the fisheye field
of view beyond \SI{70}{\degree} (\cref{sec:exp1-real}).

\subsection{RTAB-Map mapping and localization back end}
\label{sec:rtab-method}

RTAB-Map does not measure motion itself: it takes the front end's pose and
adds memory, remembering places, recognizing them in two stages (visual words,
then geometry) and building the grid Nav2 plans on (\cref{fig:rtab-pipeline}).
In mapping mode it writes the prior database; in localization mode it only
reads it. It receives front-end odometry through TF and stereo imagery through the
camera shim, which reconstructs \texttt{CameraInfo}, decompresses
the pair, and publishes validated static camera transforms from the common
simulation calibration. TF was selected over an odometry topic because adding
high-rate odometry to multi-topic approximate synchronization starved the
lower-rate images. The cost is that odometry covariance is not supplied to graph
edge weighting.

  the camera shim (RTAB-1) and the front end's pose (RTAB-2) feed a node every

  \figsource{adapted from Labb\'e and Michaud, 2019}
  \label{fig:rtab-pipeline}

The back end uses stereo SGBM (\repo{Stereo/DenseStrategy: 1}), height-based
ground separation, ray tracing, spatial noise filtering, and odometry-derived
gravity links. Its measured default depth range is \SI{10}{\metre}. Because the
featureless floor provides essentially no stereo ground points, ray tracing
supplies free-space evidence between the sensor and observed obstacles. These
settings affect occupancy assembly; visual-word loop closure is separate.

Three modes are provided by one launch file
(\cref{sec:nav-integration} describes how they are used):
\begin{description}
  \item[construct] starts a new incremental database;
  \item[extend] opens an existing database, places prior nodes in working memory,
    and permits new nodes; and
  \item[localize] disables incremental memory, loads existing nodes, serves the
    saved map, and can enable \repo{Mem/LocalizationReadOnly}.
\end{description}

Localization still extracts features from current stereo images and matches
stored RTAB-Map signatures. Read-only mode prevents database modification; it
does not disable perception. The outputs are an external
\texttt{map~$\rightarrow$~odom} correction and \repo{/localization_pose}. Neither
RTAB-Map features nor corrected poses are injected into ORB-SLAM3's Atlas or
VINS-Fusion's sliding window.

The composed localization launch raises \repo{RGBD/OptimizeMaxError} from 3.0 to
10.0 because the default rejected all loop closures proposed with the observed
VINS drift. This is effective operationally but is not yet proven safe against
perceptual aliasing in repeated warehouse bays. Occupancy creation is disabled in
localization mode because the stored map is reused. Running the back end live
beside its front end works with ORB-SLAM3, on this computer with active cooling,
and every relocalization run of Experiment~2 is made that way. With VINS-Fusion
it does not: VINS-Fusion diverged when co-run with RTAB-Map from a bag, and no
successful co-run is recorded. Its recorded trajectory is replayed as
\texttt{odom~$\rightarrow$~vio\_body} instead, which gives a deterministic
back-end test but is not evidence of real-time co-execution.

\subsection{Navigation and relocalization integration: the
\repo{vslam_navigation} package}
\label{sec:nav-integration}

The bring-up of the three components above is collected into one ROS~2 package,
\repo{vslam_navigation}, the instrument used to answer the second half of
Experiment~2's question: whether the robot relocalizes in the prior map well
enough to hand Nav2 a usable pose. The two front ends and the mapping back end are separate processes with
different frame conventions, different image transports and, on the real rig, a
different camera model; operating them by hand produced several invalid runs.
Path planning remains outside the scope of this project (\cref{sec:scope}): the
planner appears only as the consumer that makes the localization's usability
measurable. \Cref{chap:results} reports how well the robot relocalizes while
it is driven through the map; no goal is commanded and no planning result is
reported.

\subsubsection{Pose by transform rather than by topic}

The back end is not told which front end produced its pose. RTAB-Map is given an
\texttt{odom\_frame\_id} and reads the sensor pose from the transform tree rather
than from an odometry message, logging \texttt{subscribe\_odom = false}
accordingly. One back-end configuration therefore accepts ORB-SLAM3 or
VINS-Fusion unchanged. Each front end is told which frame names to broadcast,
which avoids bridging three naming conventions with static identity transforms.
The resulting chain has exactly one publisher per edge in both domains
(\cref{sec:frames-contract}).

\subsubsection{Manufacturing the stereo input}

RTAB-Map's stereo pipeline requires a rectified image pair, both camera
information messages and a static transform to the optical frames, and the
recordings carry only compressed images. The camera shim constructs the
missing inputs from the same calibration files the estimators
read, so a single calibration remains the source of truth. The two domains need
different treatment:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simulation.} A Gazebo camera is an ideal pinhole: every
    distortion coefficient is zero and the two cameras share intrinsics, so the
    raw frames already satisfy what rectification would produce and are
    republished unaltered. This shortcut is valid only in simulation.
  \item \textbf{Real rig.} The cameras are approximately \SI{180}{\degree}
    Kannala--Brandt fisheyes (\cref{sec:real-calibration}), and RTAB-Map's
    stereo path has no equidistant model. Every pair is therefore remapped onto
    a virtual rectified pinhole pair of stated horizontal field of view. The
    rectification rotations come from \texttt{cv2.fisheye.stereoRectify}, but
    the projection matrices are constructed explicitly, because OpenCV's own
    projection degenerates towards zero focal length for a lens of this field of
    view. The remap is validated on-line by an epipolar statistic: the fraction
    of cross-checked ORB matches whose rectified rows agree to within two pixels
    at positive disparity.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Three memory modes, and why a prior must be built without
closures}

The back end is operated in one of three modes: \emph{construct} writes a new
database, \emph{extend} appends a session to an existing one, and
\emph{localize} runs read-only against a prior with RTAB-Map's incremental
memory disabled. Priors from the two mapping recordings are built
\emph{closure-free}: both detectors that can add a non-neighbour link are shut,
the appearance-based one by raising its acceptance threshold beyond reach and
the proximity one by disabling it. The reason is measured: on those recordings
the accepted closures were false and displaced the map by metres
(\cref{sec:map-realistic}). Visual words are still extracted, so the database
remains usable for localization; the cheaper alternative of disabling feature
extraction would yield a database that can never relocalize.

RTAB-Map restores its parameter set \emph{from the database}, so a closure-free
prior returns with both detectors still disabled even when the launch requests
closures. Relocalizing against a prior \emph{is} the acceptance of a loop
closure against it, so in localize mode the effect is fatal and silent: the node
starts, publishes a localization pose, and never localizes. The symptom is a
covariance trace pinned at RTAB-Map's no-information value of
\num{3e4}, against roughly \num{0.02} for a successful fix. The launch now
sets both parameters explicitly, since a run-time parameter overrides the stored
one.

\subsubsection{Gating the planner on the localization}

A supervisor, the pose gate, holds the planner's
lifecycle nodes inactive until three conditions hold together: an initialization
interval has elapsed, the correction between the map and odometry frames has
moved less than a stated step over a number of consecutive samples, and the pose
lies inside the prior graph's node bounds plus a margin. The gate exists because
nothing in the localization path reports its own failure, and a planner acting
on a wrong pose is worse than one that does not act. It activates the stack
through the lifecycle manager and pauses it again if the conditions stop
holding. The same boolean is consumed by the
command interface, so a lost localization zeroes the robot's commands rather
than merely stopping replanning.

\subsubsection{Planner, controller and vehicle model}
\label{sec:nav-algorithms}

The planning layer is Nav2~\cite{macenski2020} with three components chosen for a
car rather than for a differential-drive robot: a kinematic search planner, a
pursuit controller, and a velocity smoother that preserves path curvature. None
is a contribution of this project; the configuration is reported because it
decides whether a plan on the reconstructed map is one the vehicle can follow.

One constraint governs every value in \cref{tab:nav-params}. A car cannot turn
on the spot, so for a kinematic bicycle of wheel base $L$ at steering angle
$\delta$ the rear axle traces a circle of curvature
\begin{equation}
  \kappa = \frac{\tan \delta}{L},
  \qquad
  \kappa_{\max} = \frac{\tan \delta_{\max}}{L},
  \label{eq:curvature-bound}
\end{equation}
and the bound is independent of speed. The planned frame is not the rear axle,
however, so the radius that the planner must respect is the larger
$R_b = \sqrt{R^2 + d^2}$, with $R = 1/\kappa_{\max}$ and $d$ the longitudinal
offset of \texttt{base\_link} from the rear axle. Confusing the two radii
produces plans the controller cannot track, and the steering plugin does not
reject an infeasible command: it clamps the radius internally and the vehicle
silently under-turns.

The planner is the Hybrid-A* variant distributed with Nav2, which searches the
vehicle's kinematic state space rather than a grid of free cells and then
refines the result~\cite{dolgov2010}. Its motion primitives are Dubins
curves~\cite{dubins1957}, so every expansion is a path the vehicle could drive,
and the search is forward-only. Reverse primitives are available but not
enabled: the rig carries a forward-facing stereo pair, so reversing would move
the robot in the one direction its sensors do not observe. The planner's
minimum turning radius is set above the kinematic floor rather than at it,
leaving the controller authority to correct tracking error; planning at the
exact limit makes any lateral error unrecoverable.

The controller is Regulated Pure Pursuit~\cite{macenski2023rpp}, which bounds
the commanded curvature geometrically rather than by tuning. It steers towards a
carrot at lookahead distance $L_d$, emitting
\begin{equation}
  \kappa = \frac{2y}{L_d^{\,2}}, \qquad |y| \le L_d
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  |\kappa| \le \frac{2}{L_d},
  \label{eq:rpp-curvature}
\end{equation}
so a lookahead of at least $2R_b$ guarantees that no command exceeds what the
vehicle can steer. That is why the lookahead distances in
\cref{tab:nav-params} are long, and why shortening them to tighten cornering is
counterproductive: it raises the curvature the controller may ask for.
Rotate-to-heading is disabled because it commands a yaw rate at zero speed,
which the bicycle model turns into full steering lock at a standstill. The
velocity smoother scales all velocity components by one factor, so reducing
speed leaves $\kappa = \omega_z/v_x$ and therefore the demanded steering angle
unchanged.

Both costmaps carry the prior map and an inflation layer, and no live obstacle
layer in either domain. The simulated rig has no lidar and the real one has none
either, and the back end's local occupancy grids are disabled in localize mode
because the dense stereo cost starved the front end. Anything that moved into
the environment after the map was built is therefore invisible to the planner,
which is a limitation of the instrument and not a property of the map
(\cref{sec:map-eval-method}).

\subsubsection{Command interface}

The two domains differ structurally, not by tuning. The simulated robot's
steering plugin consumes a velocity command directly, so the planner's output
reaches it unchanged. The real rig accepts a speed and a steering angle on
separate topics, so a command bridge inverts the bicycle model,
\begin{equation}
  \delta = \arctan\!\left(\frac{L\,\omega_z}{v_x}\right),
  \qquad |\delta| \le \delta_{\max},
  \label{eq:bicycle-inverse}
\end{equation}
with $L$ the wheel base and $\delta_{\max}$ the steering limit. Three
behaviours in that node are consequences of the platform rather than choices.
The standstill case of
\cref{sec:nav-algorithms} is guarded a second time here: steering is forced to
zero below a speed threshold.
Both outputs are zeroed when no command has arrived within a timeout, so that a
planner crash mid-goal cannot leave the last command executing. The published
speed is also capped in the robot's own command units, which are not
metres per second and whose scale is known only at a single operating point
(\flag{the speed scale is provisional; \cref{sec:real-platform}}).

\subsubsection{What relocalization does, and what it does not}
\label{sec:reloc-limits}

Relocalization as implemented is \emph{known-start}, not arbitrary-start. The
launch argument \repo{start_pose} defaults to \texttt{origin}, the dock the
prior was recorded from, and the alternatives are the pose left in the database
or six numbers given in the map frame. The run is therefore
seeded with an approximate pose and corrected from it; nothing in the pipeline
recovers a pose from no prior information, and no claim of arbitrary-start
localization is made anywhere in this report.

Two ways of producing a prior that can never be localized against have been
found and closed. The first is a closure-free database, which restores
\repo{Rtabmap/LoopThr} to 1.0 on load so that no appearance match can ever be
accepted. The second is a database whose visual vocabulary is absent: the
\texttt{Feature} table stores each keypoint's word identifier, but only the
\texttt{Word} table stores the descriptors that a query is matched against, and
one prior was measured holding 122 \texttt{Word} rows
against the 35\,683 distinct word identifiers its features referenced. Every
pose from such a prior carries RTAB-Map's no-information covariance, trace
\num{3e4}, and no fix is ever accepted. The fault is not a consequence of
building without closures, since priors built earlier the same week are intact;
the launch now reads both counts before starting and refuses the prior rather
than letting a run discover it.

The remaining limitation is orientation at the first fix, and it is partly
closed. A fix taken while the front end is still settling latches the map frame
\SIrange{73}{76}{\degree} wrong and holds that correction for roughly
\SI{16}{\second}; holding the back end back through the transient with
\repo{localize_delay_s} removes it, and the largest correction yaw falls from
\SIrange{110}{177}{\degree} to \SI{25}{\degree} (\cref{sec:exp4-sim}). What
remains is that residual, because the warehouse aisles present the same rack
faces from several places and an appearance match can be accepted at the wrong
one.

report uses. It is short because the sweep that produced it rejected more than
it adopted: of the five acceptance controls tried, four were either inert or
destructive, so the configuration departs from RTAB-Map's own defaults in
two parameters, with a third measure applied at the launch rather than in the
library.

\subsection{Simulation fidelity and its limits}
\label{sec:sim-fidelity}

Every simulation result rests on how faithfully the simulator reproduces the
sensors and the contact physics; this subsection states what is idealized and
in which direction each idealization biases the comparison.

\paragraph{Inertial noise.} The IMU noise is declared in the robot model, so
the simulator's own configuration is the ground truth that a measurement from
the recordings tries to recover (\cref{tab:imu-noise-assumed}). The simulated
IMU is about twice as noisy as the EuRoC reference on both channels, so it is
not an optimistic sensor. The visual estimators are not told that noise: both
run on inherited defaults, five to six times the injected densities and 58
times the injected gyroscope bias walk, which is standard practice in
visual-inertial odometry but is not a description of this IMU. The measured
accelerometer density is four to six times the injected one because the
two-second stationary windows cannot separate noise from the robot's own
acceleration, and the bias walks cannot be measured at all; the declared values
are therefore the ones to use. The EKF baseline runs on the same configured
values (\cref{sec:proprio-method}), so the two families are compared at a
shared handicap. The IMU's orientation field carries no noise at
all and is never consumed.

\paragraph{Camera, chassis and physics.} The camera, at about 1.3 grey levels
of noise in 255 and with no distortion, is much cleaner than any real one;
chassis vibration, IMU scale-factor and cross-axis errors and temperature drift
are absent. The physics engine is Gazebo Fortress's default, \repo{dartsim},
at a \SI{1}{\milli\second} step with every solver parameter at its default;
the declared slip parameters are an ODE feature that \repo{dartsim} ignores.
The wheel encoders report the true joint angle, so slip would appear as an
over-read, but measured slip is below \SI{0.4}{\percent} on all four
recordings, against a wheel-odometry drift of \SIrange{2.2}{5.9}{\percent} of
route length; encoder quantization is not modelled.

\begin{queried}[Threat to validity]
This is a bias with a known direction. The non-visual baselines of
\cref{sec:results-proprio} run on a floor where the wheels effectively cannot
slip and with an encoder of unlimited resolution, which is dead reckoning's
best case. Both idealizations favour the proprioceptive estimators over the
visual ones, and neither has been relaxed, so any conclusion that a
wheel-plus-IMU filter matches the visual pipelines holds \emph{under these
conditions}, not in general.
\end{queried}

\section{The Two Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Each experiment below is defined by its objective, its process, its evaluation
metrics, its constraints and the validity rule that decides whether a run
counts. Each is run in both domains; \cref{chap:results} reports each domain in
its own slot.

\subsection{Experiment 1: Estimator comparison against a non-visual baseline}
\label{sec:proprio-method}

\textbf{Objective.} Establish a dead-reckoning baseline to determine whether the
visual pipelines (ORB-SLAM3 and VINS-Fusion) genuinely outperform the robot's
own wheel odometry, its IMU, and a wheel-plus-IMU extended Kalman filter, and
whether the uncertainty each estimator reports describes the error it makes.

\textbf{Process.} Wheel odometry, IMU dead reckoning and the EKF are run
offline on the same recordings the visual estimators consumed, from
proprioceptive topics only. A calibration pass measures each sensor's noise and
scale against ground truth first; the estimators then run and are scored on the
same interval as the visual runs.

\textbf{Evaluation metrics.} Aligned and anchored ATE, final drift as a fraction
of distance, final heading error, RPE at \SI{1}{\second}, and the covariance
consistency measures NEES and $k\sigma$ coverage.

\textbf{Validity rule.} A run counts only if the planar assumption holds
(maximum tilt below \SI{1}{\degree}), the IMU orientation field is not consumed,
and the number of wheel corrections equals the number of wheel-odometry
messages.

Three non-visual estimators supply the dead-reckoning floor: wheel odometry, IMU
dead reckoning, and an extended Kalman filter fusing the two. They read the
recorded bag directly and are deterministic functions of it, which keeps replay
rate and CPU contention out of the comparison; the visual figures they are
compared against come from live runs, an asymmetry stated here once.

\textbf{Visual estimators.} Accuracy is measured on the four
\repo{dataset_allsensor_realistic} recordings, one run per cell, with the
estimator, the feature extractor (CPU or GPU) and the loop closer (active or
disabled) as factors, each replayed live at rate 1.0. Latency and resources are
measured on the two mapping recordings, three runs per configuration, so that a
spread exists. Metrics are ATE and rotational error after rigid alignment, RPE
at \SI{1}{\second} (all pairs and jump-free), tracking-loss and reset events,
retained input, CPU, peak memory and per-module latency. A step implying more
than \SI{10}{\metre\per\second} is a jump, not drift; an indoor error above
\SI{50}{\metre} is divergence; and a timing comparison is admissible only when
its runs are interleaved from a cool start, because the laptop throttles at
\SI{90}{\celsius}.

\subsubsection{EKF formulation}
\label{sec:ekf-formulation}

\paragraph{State and inputs.} The filter carries five planar states,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix}p_x & p_y & \theta & v & b_\omega\end{bmatrix}^{\top},
  \label{eq:ekf-state}
\end{equation}
where $b_\omega$ is the gyroscope $z$ bias. Roll and pitch stay within
\SI{0.002}{\degree} across every recording, so a full 15-state inertial
formulation would spend nine states on quantities this platform does not have,
and their covariances would be unobservable noise in the consistency test that
motivates the experiment.

The IMU is not a measurement here: it drives the prediction. Bias-corrected yaw
rate and longitudinal acceleration act as the input,
\begin{equation}
  \omega = \omega_{\text{gyro}} - b_\omega, \qquad a = a_x - b_a,
  \label{eq:ekf-input}
\end{equation}
with $b_a$ a constant measured during calibration rather than a tracked state.
Propagation uses the midpoint heading $\theta_m = \theta + \tfrac{1}{2}\omega\,\Delta t$,
which matters over the \SIrange{103}{171}{\metre} these recordings cover:
\begin{align}
  p_x &\mathrel{+}= v\cos\theta_m\,\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}a\cos\theta_m\,\Delta t^2, \notag\\
  p_y &\mathrel{+}= v\sin\theta_m\,\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}a\sin\theta_m\,\Delta t^2, \notag\\
  v   &\mathrel{+}= a\,\Delta t, \qquad \theta \mathrel{+}= \omega\,\Delta t.
  \label{eq:ekf-propagate}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Covariance and process noise.} With $\mathbf{F}=\partial f/\partial
\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{G}$ mapping the noise inputs into the state,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{P} \leftarrow \mathbf{F}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{F}^{\top}
    + \mathbf{G}\,\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_a^2,\ \sigma_g^2,\ \sigma_{b}^2)\,\mathbf{G}^{\top}.
  \label{eq:ekf-predict}
\end{equation}
The non-trivial entries are
$F_{13}=-v\sin\theta_m\Delta t$, $F_{14}=\cos\theta_m\Delta t$,
$F_{23}=v\cos\theta_m\Delta t$, $F_{24}=\sin\theta_m\Delta t$ and
$F_{35}=-\Delta t$; the last couples bias error directly into heading, which is
what makes $b_\omega$ worth estimating. In $\mathbf{G}$, accelerometer noise
enters position as $\tfrac{1}{2}\cos\theta_m\Delta t^2$ and speed as $\Delta t$,
gyroscope noise enters heading as $\Delta t$, and the bias row carries
$\sqrt{\Delta t}$ rather than $\Delta t$ because a random walk accumulates as
$\sqrt{t}$.

Both IMU noise terms are therefore \emph{process} noise. Nothing from the IMU
appears in $\mathbf{R}$, because the IMU propagates the state rather than
observing it.

\paragraph{Measurement.} The wheels correct at \SI{50}{\hertz} with
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{z} = \begin{bmatrix}v_{\text{wheel}} \\
    \omega_{\text{gyro}} - \omega_{\text{wheel}}\end{bmatrix}, \qquad
  \mathbf{H} = \begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&1&0\\ 0&0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad
  \mathbf{R} = \mathrm{diag}\!\left(\sigma_{v,w}^2,\ \sigma_{\omega,w}^2+\sigma_g^2\right).
  \label{eq:ekf-update}
\end{equation}
The second variance is a sum because that observation is a \emph{difference} of
two noisy sensors and inherits both. The update uses the Joseph form,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{P} \leftarrow (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}\mathbf{H})\mathbf{P}
    (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}\mathbf{H})^{\top} + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{K}^{\top},
  \label{eq:ekf-joseph}
\end{equation}
symmetrized afterwards. The shorter $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}\mathbf{H})\mathbf{P}$
does not reliably stay positive definite across the thousands of updates in a
run, and here the covariance is itself the measurement under test, so it cannot
be allowed to decay.

\paragraph{Where the numbers come from.} $\sigma_{v,w}$ and
$\sigma_{\omega,w}$ come from the wheel stream against ground truth
(\cref{tab:proprio-noise}); $\sigma_a$ and $\sigma_g$ come from the visual
configuration, as explained above. \moved{$\sigma_b$, the bias random walk, is
assumed rather than measured: the longest stationary window in any recording is
\SI{2.16}{\second} and an Allan-variance estimate needs minutes.}

\subsubsection{Why the bias, and not the heading, is observed}

The EKF's second measurement is deliberately an observation of the \emph{gyroscope
bias} and not of heading. Integrating the wheel yaw rate into a heading and
correcting $\theta$ with it would reproduce the wheel estimator inside the filter
and inherit its unbounded drift. Treating the instantaneous rate difference as an
observation of $b_\omega$ keeps each sensor supplying what it measures well: the
wheels know rate, the gyroscope knows change, and neither knows absolute heading,
so heading remains unobservable and its variance correctly continues to grow.

Measured scale errors are recorded but never divided out. Correcting them would
mean calibrating the estimator with the ground truth it is about to be scored
against; the scale error is a real property of encoder odometry and is retained.

\subsubsection{The real robot: a different baseline}

None of the three estimators above can be run on the real recordings. The rig
publishes no wheel odometry in metric units and no joint states, so there is no
wheel speed to integrate and no steering angle to put through the bicycle model;
the wheelbase, \SI{0.60}{\metre}, is known but has nothing to multiply. What the
bags do carry is a firmware-unit speed feedback and a gyroscope
(\cref{sec:dataset-registry}), which support one estimator:
\begin{equation}
  \theta(t) = \theta_0 + \int (\omega - b)\,\mathrm{d}t, \qquad
  \begin{bmatrix}p_x\\p_y\end{bmatrix} \mathrel{+}=
  k\,v_{\text{fb}} \begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta\\ \sin\theta\end{bmatrix} \Delta t,
  \label{eq:real-deadreckon}
\end{equation}
with $b$ the gyroscope bias measured over the stationary head of each recording
and $k$ the unknown speed scale. The baseline fits $k$, the gyroscope axis and its sign on one recording by minimizing the planar ATE
after rigid $SE(2)$ alignment, then holds all three on the others: the fit
recording's score is a calibration and only the held recordings are results.
Because no yaw-rate observation exists, there is no bias state to correct and
the EKF of \cref{eq:ekf-update} degenerates to this integration, so no filter
row is reported in the real domain.

\begin{queried}[One tuned constant]
The real baseline has a parameter fitted against the same tracker it is scored
on, albeit on a different recording. A measured scale, from the wheel
circumference and the gear ratio or from a tape-measured straight run, would
remove the objection; until then the real-domain comparison between this
baseline and the visual pipelines is weaker than its simulated counterpart.
\end{queried}


\subsection{Experiment 2: Visual-inertial odometry on a prior map}
\label{sec:exp2-method}

\textbf{Objective.} Determine whether the prior map RTAB-Map builds reflects
the warehouse, and whether the robot relocalizes in that map accurately and
continuously enough to hand Nav2 a usable pose. The experiment has two parts
that share one artefact: part~A scores the map as geometry, part~B scores the
localization the same map supports.

\subsubsection{Part A: the prior map}
\label{sec:map-eval-method}

\textbf{Process.} RTAB-Map builds a database from a mapping run; the occupancy
grid is seated in the world by a fixed transform measured from the map alone;
geometry is scored against the world's collision meshes; drivability is scored
by stamping the true robot footprint along a ground-truth route that did not
build the map.

\textbf{Evaluation metrics.} Occupied-cell precision, recall over observed
surfaces and over the whole building, false-obstacle rate under the footprint,
unmapped footprint poses, clearance statistics, and the graph's node and link
counts.

\textbf{Validity rule.} Placement must be established independently before any
metric is read, and a drivability figure computed against the run that built
the map is a self-consistency check, not a result.

The placement is one fixed rigid world-to-map transform per database, fitted
to the world's walls and obstacles and never taken from the live
\texttt{map~$\rightarrow$~odom} correction. On parallel aisles the fit has a
rival \SI{180}{\degree} away that can score higher, so a placement is accepted
only after its overlay is read. A database is always probed with a recording
that did not build it, because the build route is drivable on its own map by
construction. Three databases are evaluated: the tiny-warehouse map, and the two
realistic-warehouse priors part~B relocalizes on, each probed with the static
twin of its layout (\cref{sec:map-quality}).

\subsubsection{Part B: relocalization on the prior map}
\label{sec:nav-eval-method}

\textbf{Process.} The three stages of \repo{vslam_navigation}
(\cref{tab:nav-integration}) are run in order on a prior already scored in
part~A, so that map quality and localization refer to the same artefact:
\texttt{mapping\_sim} builds the prior, \texttt{localize\_sim} gives a
read-only fix against it, and \texttt{navigate\_sim} adds the planner, the
localization gate and the command interface. The simulator is driven live
rather than replayed, because the published pose depends on the control loop.
In the runs reported the robot is driven by the operator along fixed routes,
and the same data are recorded in the planner-driven mode.

\textbf{Evaluation metrics.} Error is the planar distance between the ground
truth and the published transform from \texttt{map} to \texttt{base\_link}, both
sampled at \SI{2}{\hertz}; no lever arm is applied, because the published
\texttt{base\_link} sits at the model origin the ground truth reports. That
transform is what Nav2 consumes, and it includes the front end's dead
reckoning between accepted fixes. Per run it is reported as a median, a 95th percentile, a
maximum and the time spent beyond \SI{1}{\metre}, and split into a constant
offset and the scatter about it. Availability is the share of samples with a
no-information covariance, and continuity the number of pose steps over
\SI{1}{\metre} between consecutive samples (\cref{tab:targets}). Heading error
and the RTAB-Map place-recognition proposals, acceptances and rejections are
recorded beside them.

\textbf{Validity rule.} The availability and continuity thresholds and the goal
set were fixed before the first run. A run
that loses localization is reported, not excluded. Samples before the first
accepted fix are excluded, because before it the transform is the seed and not
an estimate.

\textbf{Not measured.} No goal was commanded in the runs reported, so the
protocol's goal-reaching measures --- share of goals reached within
\SI{1.0}{\metre} and \SI{30}{\degree}, path length against the shortest
collision-free path, collisions with true structure and recovery behaviours ---
remain planned.

\section{Evaluation Metrics and Analysis}
\label{sec:evaluation-method}

\subsection{Trajectory evaluation}
\label{sec:method-trajectory}
Estimate and reference are associated over their common interval, with the
reference interpolated at the estimator's timestamps. A rigid Umeyama $SE(3)$
fit with scale fixed at one maps the estimate onto the reference --- fixed scale
is right for stereo-inertial estimators and preserves any scale error --- and a
planar estimate is fitted by a turn about the vertical only. ATE is the RMSE of
the aligned positions; rotational error is the geodesic angle after the same
fit. Relative pose error over a fixed interval $\Delta$ uses
\begin{equation}
  E_i = \left(Q_i^{-1}Q_{i+k}\right)^{-1}\left(P_i^{-1}P_{i+k}\right),
  \label{eq:rpe}
\end{equation}
with $P$ the estimate and $Q$ the reference; translational RPE is
$\lVert\operatorname{trans}(E_i)\rVert$ and rotational RPE the geodesic angle
of $\operatorname{rot}(E_i)$.

\paragraph{Planar reference.} The real recordings carry an optical-tracker
reference that is planar, has no heading, runs on its own clock and follows a
marker rather than the camera. Alignment is therefore the rigid $SE(2)$ case
of the fit above, ATE is evaluated in the plane at the tracker's samples, and
RPE is translational only. The clock offset is estimated by cross-correlating
the tracker's speed with the bag's wheel-speed feedback, independent of both
estimators, and accepted only when the peak is sharp. The marker lever arm is
unmeasured, so every real-robot result is labelled provisional. A run is
\emph{completed} when it shuts down gracefully with no reset or jump and at
least \SI{80}{\percent} of tracker samples matched, \emph{partial} when it
recovers from a reset or falls short on coverage, and \emph{failed} on
divergence, a reset storm, coverage below \SI{20}{\percent}, or a vertical
range above \SI{1}{\metre} on a floor run (a rule added after a run that
satisfied every earlier one while climbing \SI{22}{\metre}).

\subsection{Computational performance and provenance}
\label{sec:collection-validity}
Both estimators write per-frame timing, delivery and backlog counters, tracking
state, reset evidence, cumulative CPU time and resident memory, so CPU use and
throughput are derived over explicit wall-time intervals; the measurements are
process-local and exclude the simulator, the bag player and RTAB-Map. Module
timings from asynchronous stages are reported as separate distributions, not
summed into one critical path. Every run writes its trajectory, its resolved
configuration and its event logs to its own directory, and the configurations
under comparison run separately on the same recording, never at the same time.
